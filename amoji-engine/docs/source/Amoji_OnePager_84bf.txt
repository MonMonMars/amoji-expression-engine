AMOJI
A Bridge Between AI and Human — Expression & Behavior Engine for AI Characters and Robots
The Problem
AI characters, digital humans, and social robots increasingly need to express emotion — but most systems either detect emotion (input side) or apply generic, static expression templates. Very few systems generate psychologically grounded, time-aware, personality-consistent facial performance. The result: AI faces that feel flat, robotic, or "uncanny" during pauses, transitions, and processing delays.
The Solution
A layered engine that converts an AI-generated "script" (dialogue + emotion + mood) into full facial and body performance data — combining professional animation expertise (20 years of character facial performance) with published psychology, performance-capture, and uncanny-valley research:
Compound Emotion Engine — blends two base emotions by facial region (e.g. "happily surprised"), based on published FACS research rather than naive averaging.
Mood Engine — a slow-moving background state that subtly biases and "leaks" into every displayed emotion, without explicit AI instruction.
Temporal Authenticity Layer — natural blinking, micro-expression leakage (eyes-before-mouth timing), and Alba Emoting–inspired "step-out" transitions between emotions.
Eye Anchor System — gaze-target tracking with layered microsaccades, independently matching the technique Naughty Dog's lead animator described using on The Last of Us Part II.
Latency Bridging System — persona-aware filler dialogue and gaze-aversion behavior that fills AI/robot processing delays naturally, grounded in HRI filler-word research.
Persona Parameter System — five trait weights (warmth, formality, expressiveness, playfulness, assertiveness) mathematically derive intensity caps, allowed emotions, and behavior for any character — no per-character redesign needed.
Validated by Industry Precedent
Two independent findings from AAA game and robotics production confirm our core design philosophy:
The Last of Us (2013) used zero facial motion capture — every facial performance was hand-keyed by animators studying reference footage — and remains one of gaming's most emotionally convincing character performances. Raw capture fidelity is not what makes a face believable; trained animator judgment is. This is the same principle behind Amoji's rule-based engine.
Engineered Arts (maker of Ameca, the most widely deployed hyper-realistic robot) runs two product lines: Ameca, which deliberately keeps skin tone and texture visibly artificial while making motion hyper-realistic, and Mesmer, which pursues full human realism — and by the company's own account, Mesmer "falls right into the uncanny valley." Same company, same era: consistent stylization wins, half-measures toward full realism lose.
Why It's Different
Current emotion-AI companies (e.g. Hume AI, Affectiva) focus on detecting emotion from input. Character-AI engines (e.g. Inworld, Convai) focus on dialogue generation and NPC behavior. Digital human platforms (e.g. Soul Machines) focus on rendering fidelity. None of the reviewed competitors combine psychologically-grounded compound emotion generation, mood-based leakage, and performance-trained transition timing into a single, persona-configurable engine.
